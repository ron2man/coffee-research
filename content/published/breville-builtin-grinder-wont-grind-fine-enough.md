Don't buy anything yet. If your {{product:barista-express}} or {{product:barista-pro}} still pours fast, sour and thin at grind setting 1, start with the checks that cost nothing. The manual backs every one of them: fresh beans, the right basket, a full dose, clean burrs, and then moving the upper burr finer one number at a time. Do them in order and stop at the first one that slows the shot. If none of them works, the built-in grinder has reached its limit, and the thing to buy is a separate grinder, not a new machine.

First, what setting 1 actually means. On the Express the dial runs from 1 (finest) to 16 (coarsest), and the manufacturer suggests starting at 5. On the Pro it runs from 1 to 30, with 15 as the default. If you're on 1, you've run out of dial, but not out of grinder. There's more adjustment inside, which comes later.

If you'd rather take it one question at a time, the troubleshooter below stops at your next step. The rest of this page explains each step.

## The test you can run tonight

No tools, nothing to take apart. Make one shot and check four things.

**The bag.** Look for a "Roasted On" date. A "Best Before" date doesn't count. The manual recommends using beans 5–20 days after roasting. The Pro booklet says outright that stale coffee "may pour too quickly from the portafilter and taste bitter and watery", which describes your problem exactly. If your bag has no roast date, or it's well past 20 days, buy a fresh bag before you go any further.

**The basket.** The machine comes with two kinds. Single-wall baskets are for fresh whole beans. Dual-wall (pressurised) baskets are for pre-ground coffee. In the manual's words, they "regulate the pressure and help to optimize the extraction regardless of the grind, dose and tamp pressure or freshness." With a dual-wall basket fitted, the basket is doing the work, so changing the grind tells you very little. Diagnose with a single-wall basket.

**The dose.** Tamp firmly (the manual says 22–33 lb, roughly 10–15 kg of pressure), then check where the tamper's metal cap sits. It should be level with the basket rim. If it sits below the rim, you're under-dosing. A double basket is designed for about 16–19 g. On the Pro, the manual adds that a finer grind in a single-wall basket may need more coffee.

**The timing.** Start a timer when you press the button and note when the espresso starts to flow. The Pro's guide says 8–12 seconds is normal, and anything under 7 seconds means too little coffee or too coarse a grind. The Express guide describes an under-extracted shot as one that starts flowing after 1–6 seconds and finishes within 20, against a target of 25–30 seconds.

The order comes from the manufacturer. Both troubleshooting tables give the same three causes for "Espresso runs out too quickly": grind too coarse, not enough coffee, tamped too lightly. Neither lists the burrs, which is why the burrs come last here.

## Clean the burrs before you move them

Fresh beans, a single-wall basket and a full dose, and the shot still gushes? Clean the grinder. The manual's procedure is to empty the hopper, run the grinder empty, take out the top burr, and brush both burrs and the chute. The manual says this "removes oil build-up on the burrs which can impede performance." It's free and takes a few minutes, and you have to take the top burr out for the next step anyway. Skip the advanced clean that removes the lower burr; the manual says it's only for blockages. Both booklets also warn that the burrs are extremely sharp, so hold them by the edges.

## Move the upper burr toward fine, one number at a time

Forum threads are full of "never touch the internal burr" and "shim it." The manual says something different. Both booklets call the adjustable upper burr "a feature" of the machine that extends the grind range, because "some types of coffee may require a wider grind range."

The procedure, condensed from the booklet:

1. Unlock the hopper, empty the beans into a container, refit the hopper and run the grinder until it's empty.
2. Remove the hopper, then lift out the upper burr by its wire handle. On the Express the booklet shows no tools for this, and the Allen key in the box is for the shower-screen seal.
3. Unclip the wire handle, write down the number the burr is on now, and turn the adjustable ring **one number toward finer**.
4. Line the number up with the handle opening, refit the handle, push the burr in firmly and lock it, then refit and lock the hopper.
5. Pull a shot with the same beans and the same dose, and time it again.

The manual recommends one adjustment at a time, and so do I. If one step slows the shot a little but not enough, repeat.

A word on direction. When shots run fast, you want finer. At least one well-known retailer guide tells readers with fast shots at the finest setting to go coarser, which is the wrong way for your problem. The Pro booklet's illustrations show the ring marked FINE and COARSE, so follow the ring, not the forum.

**What the booklet doesn't say.** Neither booklet gives the range of numbers on the ring, the factory position, any warning about the burrs touching, or whether adjusting the burr affects the warranty. You'll see a factory number repeated online, but it comes from blogs, not the manufacturer, so don't rely on it. That's why you write down your starting number. My rule, which is my judgement and not the manual's: if the grinder starts to sound like metal on metal, or one more step finer makes no difference to the shot, go back one number and stop there.

If you're going finer to grind very light roasts, note that the manual warns against grinding green or under-roasted beans. It says they're too dense and not brittle enough for a burr grinder, and they can damage or break the burrs.

One owner's experience fits the pattern, though it's a single report, not a spec. In August 2026, someone on the Singletrack forum with a refurbished Pro was getting gritty, chunky grounds and only a passable shot through the pressurised basket. They cleaned the grinder, moved the internal burr from 2 to 1 with the dial at 2, and reported a 28-second shot that was "WAY better." The manufacturer also offered them a free upper burr, but the improvement came before that burr arrived.

## Still fast? Check your date code and contact the manufacturer

By now you've done everything above: fresh beans, a single-wall basket, the cap level with the rim, clean burrs, and the upper burr as fine as it goes without contact. If shots still run fast, the burr itself is the next suspect. Before buying anything, find the production date code (PDC) on the model sticker under the machine. An Australian parts seller lists two different upper burrs for these machines: SP0001801 for machines built before PDC 1926, and SP0027464 for machines from 1926 (2019, week 26) onwards. That comes from a parts listing, not a manufacturer spec, and nobody checked prices or availability anywhere else.

Contact the manufacturer's support before you order. The booklet's warranty covers repair or replacement when the machine is "defective due to faulty materials, workmanship, or function." A machine that can't grind fine enough with fresh beans could reasonably count, and at least one owner was sent a free burr. Have your date code and shot times ready.

## What to buy if none of it works

If any step above slowed the shot to a proper 25–30 seconds, buy nothing. Keep the machine, keep buying beans with a roast date, and leave the upper burr where it ended up.

If you've done every step and the shot still runs fast at setting 1, the built-in grinder is the limit. By every step I mean the burr as fine as it goes without contact, plus either no help from the manufacturer or a replacement burr that changed nothing. Don't replace the whole machine. The pump, the steam wand and the basket you've now set up aren't the problem, only the grinder is. So the one purchase that makes sense is a separate espresso grinder. Grind into the portafilter from that, and keep the built-in grinder as a backup for days when you're in a hurry. Which grinder pairs well with these two machines is its own question, and {{guide:grinder-for-barista-express-and-pro|our guide to choosing a grinder for the Barista Express and Pro}} answers it.

## When I'd choose differently

These are my calls, not the manual's.

**You mainly drink very light roasts.** You'll be working at the fine end of the built-in grinder every day, and the manual warns that under-roasted beans can damage its burrs. For you, a separate grinder makes sense sooner than for someone happy with medium roasts, even if the burr adjustment gets your shots into range. Do the free checks, then go straight to {{guide:grinder-for-barista-express-and-pro|the grinder guide}}.

**You just bought a refurbished machine and the grounds look gritty or chunky.** Run the checks, but contact the seller or the manufacturer early. The owner in the forum report was offered a free burr, and a fault on a newly bought machine is the seller's problem to fix, not yours.

**You were already planning to upgrade the machine.** If you expect to move to a machine without a grinder in the next year or so, buy the separate grinder now, because it carries over to the next machine. Do the free checks first anyway, so you know the machine wasn't the problem.

We read the current instruction booklets for the Barista Express (BES870, X26 edition) and the Barista Pro (BES878, Q26 edition) page by page. We covered grind settings, filter baskets, the extraction guide, burr cleaning, "Adjusting Conical Burrs", troubleshooting and the warranty paragraph. These are the North American editions. We also read the manufacturer's grind-size tutorial for the Pro, two Australian parts-seller listings for the upper burr, a retailer's top-burr guide, a blog post on the inner burr, and an owner thread on the Singletrack forum. We compared these sources with each other and with the booklets.

What we did not do: we did not test either machine or any burr setting. We did not ask the manufacturer's support about the warranty, and we did not check prices or availability for replacement burrs beyond the two Australian listings. We have not yet researched standalone grinders for these machines, so this guide doesn't name one. The owner report is one person's experience.
