# Coffee editorial SEO

## Choose the topic

Compare at least two plausible topics. For each, record a short primary query phrased as a reader might type it, related queries with the same intent, target market and language, search date, and the decision the reader wants to make. Group synonymous queries into one opportunity. Check existing drafts for intent overlap and consider updating an existing article when it already serves that intent.

Record demand separately from the gap in existing answers. Label demand evidence as measured, qualitative, or unmeasured. For measured data, record the provider, metric, market, and period. When available, use Search Console query impressions and clicks for existing pages. Qualitative signals include independently recurring owner questions or related search suggestions. A product launch, isolated thread, or result count alone gives weak demand evidence. Avoid estimating volume or ranking probability from these signals.

Inspect competing results for the reader query. Identify what they already answer and the specific decision your draft would improve. Compare candidates on demand evidence, the competitive gap, and availability of product evidence. Record why the winner beat the alternatives. If the angle is useful but demand is unmeasured, present it as an editorial experiment. Defer when there is no supportable contribution.

## Test the article before drafting

Write one sentence in the brief stating what the reader can decide or do after reading that they could not do from the product listings or competing results. Then show the evidence or concrete test that makes the takeaway possible. If the takeaway could apply to almost any two products after swapping their names, research the missing decision factor or choose a narrower question. For a buying comparison, that might mean checking comparable offers and warranty terms in the target market, finding an independent test of the feature that carries the premium, or giving the reader a specific test that changes which listing they would choose. Record actual observations with scope and date. If the evidence supports only a narrower answer, narrow the promise in the title and brief.

## Write and hand off

For a buying comparison, put the recommendation in the first paragraph. Say which option you would choose for the typical reader, who should choose differently, and what would change your mind. For a “worth paying more?” question, identify what the premium buys in daily use and when that benefit is worth paying for. If the price gap is unknown, give a conditional buying rule based on the reader’s routine or the actual quotes they receive. Avoid inventing a price threshold or measuring a benefit the research has only documented as a manufacturer claim.

Use the rest of the article to explain that recommendation. Translate relevant specs into consequences the reader can picture, such as whether a faster cold start changes their morning routine. Put model identifiers, seller checks, and other supporting details where they help verify the decision. Before handoff, read the opening on its own. A reader should know what you would buy in their situation and why.

- Give the article one descriptive main heading and answer the primary question early. Use H2 sections in the order a reader makes decisions and H3s to subdivide them where needed.
- Include the reader's terms naturally. Use exact product variants where relevant and avoid repeated keywords or headings that restate the title.
- Make the contribution concrete through a decision table, model distinction, or steps backed by evidence. Keep citations close to the claims.
- In the brief, provide a proposed page title, stable slug, concise meta description, and relevant internal-link targets with useful anchor text where an existing page or draft fits. Rendering metadata, link checks, and indexing belong to the website workflow.
